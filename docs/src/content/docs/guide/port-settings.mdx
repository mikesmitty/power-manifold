Each port has its own settings. You can change them in the web interface's
**Settings**, in [Home Assistant](/guide/home-assistant/), over
[MQTT](/integrations/mqtt/) or with the
[HTTP API](/integrations/http-api/). Changes apply at once,
even to a device that is already plugged in. The exception is
[Protect running device](#protect-running-device), which applies from the
next device plugged in.

## Name

Give each port a name, such as `pi-cluster-1` or `Desk phone`, up to 23
characters. The name shows in the web interface and in Home Assistant, where
the entities are renamed to match: *Port 3 power* becomes *Desk phone
power*. Renaming doesn't change any entity IDs, so your automations keep
working.

Leave the name blank to go back to `Port 1`, `Port 2` and so on.

## On or off at power-up

Choose what each port does when Power Manifold is switched on, for example
after a power cut:

- **on** (the default): the port powers up.
- **off**: the port stays off until you switch it on.
- **last**: the port comes back the way you last left it.

When the chassis powers up, the ports come on one at a time, a quarter of a
second apart, in [priority](/guide/sharing-power/#priorities)
order. This stops six devices from starting all at once.

## Voltage cap

The voltage cap is the highest voltage a port offers. Choose 5, 9, 12, 15
or 20 V. The default is 20 V, which offers everything.

The cap applies to PPS (Programmable Power Supply) as well. A port offers
two PPS ranges, 3.3–11 V and 3.3–21 V, and offers a range only when all of
it fits under the cap:

| Cap | PPS ranges offered |
|---|---|
| 5 V or 9 V | none |
| 12 V or 15 V | 3.3–11 V |
| 20 V | 3.3–11 V and 3.3–21 V |

The 20 V cap is the one exception: it still offers the 3.3–21 V range,
which goes slightly above 20 V.

Use a lower cap to protect a device that shouldn't get a high voltage. For
example:

- **5 V** for a device with a USB-C trigger board or adapter that you don't
  fully trust.
- **9 V** to limit the charging power of a phone.

## Current limit

The current limit is the most current a port offers at any voltage, from
500 to 5000 mA. Power scales with the voltage the device picks: a 3000 mA
limit means 15 W at 5 V and 60 W at 20 V.

The limit only sets what the port offers. A device that requests more
receives the limited amount and is not switched off.

Above 3 A the cable must have a 5 A e-marker chip. The port checks the cable
and offers 5 A only on a cable that can carry it. With an ordinary cable a
port stops at 3 A (60 W at 20 V), whatever the limit says.
</Aside>

## Switching off when charged

The controller decides a device has finished charging when it draws less
than **500 mW** for **10 minutes**. The port light then turns white, as if
nothing were plugged in, and the port shows *charged*. If the device starts
drawing again for that long, it counts as charging again.

Both numbers can be changed in **Settings**, as **Charged below** and
**…for**. To turn this off, set the power threshold to 0: no device is ever
marked charged, and **Switch off once charged** never turns a port off.

Two per-port options build on this:

- **Switch off once charged** turns the port off as soon as the device is
  charged. Use it to stop phones and tablets from staying at 100 %
  overnight.
- **Sleep timer** turns the port off a set number of minutes after you plug
  a device in, whether it has finished charging or not, up to 24 hours.

<ChargeTimeline />

When either one switches a port off, it stays off until you switch it on
again. A port set to **last** at power-up remembers that it was off.

<Aside type="tip">
**Switch off once charged** and the sleep timer are meant for devices with a
battery. Leave them off on ports that power a Raspberry Pi or another
computer that should stay on.
</Aside>

## Protect running device

Turn on **Protect running device** for devices that could be harmed by
potential power loss during renegotiation.

Normally, [power sharing](/guide/sharing-power/) can change what a port
offers while its device is running. It turns the port down to make room for
a higher-priority port, or limits it when the device asks for more than is
free. Each time, the device has to agree on a new contract with the port,
and some devices may briefly lose power while they do. If a device doesn't
answer the new offer quickly enough, the USB Power Delivery standard
requires the port to reset the connection, which cuts its power for a
moment.

A protected port settles its power once, when a device is plugged in:

- It sets aside everything the port offers, so the device can later ask for
  anything up to that without the offer changing.
- If that much power isn't free, lower-priority ports are turned down first.
  If there still isn't enough, the port's own offer is reduced straight
  away, before the device has started up.
- While the device stays plugged in, power sharing never changes the port's
  offer, whatever the other ports' priorities. Restarting the controller,
  for example to install an update, doesn't change it either.

The power stays set aside until the device is unplugged, even while the
device draws less. A device that was offered less because power was short
keeps that smaller offer until it is plugged in again.

Two things still change a protected port's offer: the
[input voltage dropping](/guide/sharing-power/#when-the-input-voltage-drops),
which limits every port to 3 A, and changing the port's current limit or
voltage cap yourself.

## Priority

Each port also has a priority, which decides which ports are turned down
first when devices ask for more power than the supply can give. See
[Sharing power between ports](/guide/sharing-power/).
